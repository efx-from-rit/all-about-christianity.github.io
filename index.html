Basics of Christianity

Throughout the various denominations and branches of the Christian religion, there remain a few core doctrines which all Christians can agree on.

The Holy Trinity

According to Catholicism, God is one Divine Being in three distinct, Divine Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. God is incomprehensible and beyond our finite human reasoning, yet His attributes are known through His self-revelation.

The attributes of God include omniscience, omnipotence, omnipresence, eternality, divine intellect, divine will, absolute purity, and immutability. God does not merely possess these attributes; He is these perfections. For example, God does not merely love; God is love.

Catholic theology maintains that there is one God with one divine nature, fully possessed by three distinct, coeternal Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. A person answers the question "who," while a nature answers the question "what." Each Person of the Trinity is fully divine and possesses the same divine nature and every divine attribute.

Although the Persons share the same divine nature and attributes, certain works are traditionally associated with particular Persons for the sake of our understanding. This theological principle is called the Principle of Appropriation. For example, creation is associated with the Father, wisdom with the Son, and love with the Holy Spirit. These associations do not mean that any divine work belongs exclusively to one Person.

Heresies Concerning the Trinity and Christ

The early Church defended Christian teaching against various theological and philosophical movements that it judged incompatible with the faith. These teachings are commonly discussed under the category of heresies. Notable examples include Gnosticism, Adoptionism, Modalism (Sabellianism), Arianism, and Pneumatomachianism.

Gnosticism

Gnosticism refers to a variety of ancient religious movements that emerged during the early centuries of Christianity. Their beliefs differed, although many Gnostic systems emphasized special spiritual knowledge and distinguished the spiritual realm from the material world, which some regarded as evil. Certain Gnostic groups also distinguished between the God of the Old Testament and the God revealed in the New Testament.

Docetism, associated with some early Christian and Gnostic currents, taught that Jesus only appeared to possess a human body and did not truly assume human flesh. This conflicts with the Christian teaching that Jesus Christ is truly God and truly man.

Adoptionism

Adoptionism encompasses teachings that understand Jesus as a human being who was adopted or specially appointed by God, rather than being eternally divine as the Son. Such teachings deny or depart from the traditional Christian understanding of Christ's eternal divinity. Some forms also present God as a single Person.

Modalism (Sabellianism)

Modalism teaches that God is one Person who reveals Himself in three different modes or manifestations rather than existing eternally as three distinct Persons. These modes are commonly described in connection with creation, redemption, and sanctification.

Catholic teaching rejects Modalism because the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are distinct Persons, not merely different appearances or masks assumed by a single Person.

Arianism

Arianism, associated with the teachings of Arius in the early fourth century, maintained that the Son was created by the Father and therefore was not coeternal or equal to the Father in divinity. The First Council of Nicaea, in AD 325, rejected this teaching and affirmed that the Son is consubstantial with the Father.

Pneumatomachianism

Pneumatomachians, whose name is associated with opposition to the Holy Spirit, denied or rejected the full divinity of the Holy Spirit. Catholic teaching affirms that the Holy Spirit is fully divine and belongs to the one Godhead together with the Father and the Son.

Analogies for the Holy Trinity

Every analogy used to explain the Holy Trinity has limitations. No created object or human experience can fully explain the mystery of the one God in three distinct Persons. Some common analogies can even unintentionally suggest theological errors.

  • The three roles analogy: A person may be a teacher, a parent, and a spouse. However, these are three roles of one person, not three distinct persons. This can unintentionally suggest Modalism.
  • The water analogy: Water can exist as solid ice, liquid water, and gaseous vapor. However, these are different states of one substance, not three distinct Persons who eternally share one divine nature. This analogy can also suggest Modalism.
  • The shamrock analogy: A shamrock has three leaflets, but each leaflet is only part of the whole shamrock. Applying this directly to the Trinity can suggest Partialism, the erroneous idea that each Person is only one part of God.
  • The chord analogy: Three strings played together can produce a harmonious sound. Although this can illustrate unity amid distinction, the strings do not represent divine Persons and cannot fully explain the Trinity.

These analogies may offer limited illustrations, but none adequately explains the Holy Trinity. The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are each fully God, not three parts of God or three temporary manifestations of one Person.

Meditating on the Mystery of the Trinity

The Holy Trinity is a divine mystery that surpasses the limits of human comprehension. This does not mean that we cannot know anything about God; rather, we come to know Him through His revelation while recognizing that His infinite nature exceeds our complete understanding.

We are called to contemplate this mystery throughout our lives, approaching God with reverence, humility, faith, and love.
